TopoSurface-DTI — biology primer for a mathematical physicist

Conversation saved 2026-05-08. Topic: explain the molecular biology side of the drug–target interaction project for someone with a background in algebraic topology and gauge theory.

The goal is to give the molecular intuition so the math choices (gauge equivariance on a surface, persistent homology on point clouds) make immediate sense.

1. What is a protein, geometrically?

A protein is a polymer — a one-dimensional chain of amino acids — that folds into a precise 3D shape. From a mathematical physicist's perspective, the relevant abstraction is: a protein is a smooth, oriented, compact 2-manifold (its solvent-accessible surface) embedded in R³, decorated with chemistry. The "pocket" we care about is a concavity on that manifold — locally a region of negative mean curvature.

The pocket sits on the tertiary surface and is the geometric object the GEM-CNN sees. Two facts to keep in mind:

The surface near a pocket is not flat, so a vanilla 2D CNN (which assumes a global trivialisation of the tangent bundle) cannot be applied — there is no canonical choice of "up". This is precisely the gauge ambiguity, and it is the reason the model uses irreps of SO(2) on tangent frames and parallel transport along edges. The principal SO(2)-bundle of orthonormal tangent frames is non-trivial in general; gauge-equivariant convolutions are the analogue of choosing a connection.

The pocket plus ligand together form a compact subset of R³ with non-trivial topology. Voids, tunnels, and disconnected hydrophobic patches all leave fingerprints in H₀ and H₁ of the Vietoris–Rips filtration, which is what the TDA branch picks up.

2. The drug (ligand) and what binding actually means

A drug is a small organic molecule, typically 10–60 heavy atoms. In our setup it is a labelled 3D graph (V, E, x, pos) where vertices are atoms with chemical features and positions, and edges are covalent bonds. The drug is rigid-ish but can flex; the protein's pocket can also flex slightly (induced fit).

"Binding" is the event where the drug docks into the pocket and stays there because of four non-covalent interactions:

None of these are covalent. The ligand can come and go — there is an equilibrium

P + L ⇌ PL,

with dissociation constant Kd = [P][L]/[PL]. Because Kd ranges over many orders of magnitude (millimolar to picomolar), people use pKd = −log₁₀ Kd. A "drug-like" affinity is roughly pKd ≥ 6 (Kd ≤ 1 μM); a tightly optimised drug sits at pKd ≈ 9–10.

The model's job is exactly: given the pair (pocket, ligand), predict the pKd that an experimentalist would measure.

The relationship pKd = −ΔG/(RT ln 10) is exact at 298 K, with R T ln 10 ≈ 1.364 kcal/mol per pKd unit. So predicting pKd to within 1 unit means predicting ΔG to within 1.4 kcal/mol — below the typical accuracy of even high-quality molecular dynamics. The whole point of geometric deep learning here is to short-circuit that thermodynamic calculation by recognising spatial patterns associated with strong vs. weak binding.

The label distribution in PDBBind is roughly Gaussian centred near pKd ≈ 6, range 2 to 12. So an MAE of 1.0 already does meaningful work.

4. The PDBBind dataset, geometrically

Each datapoint is an experimentally solved co-crystal structure: the protein with the ligand sitting inside its pocket, captured by X-ray diffraction. From it we extract a drug atom-graph and a protein pocket Cα-graph plus the measured pKd label.

5. Why the geometry is asymmetric

The drug is a small, flexible 3D graph with chemical labels on vertices and bonds on edges. Its geometry is essentially that of a finite weighted graph in R³. Equivariance requirement: rigid motions of R³ shouldn't change the predicted affinity. That is why DrugEncoder uses only pairwise distances expanded in radial basis functions — distances are SE(3)-invariants, so the entire encoder is invariant by construction.

The pocket is a piece of a 2-manifold, not a graph in R³. Two atoms close in Euclidean distance through the protein's interior are irrelevant; what matters is geodesic proximity on the surface itself. This is why the pocket encoder uses tangent frames and parallel transport: it does convolutions intrinsically on the surface, not in the ambient space.

For the pocket, local PCA on kNN neighbours plays the role of mesh face normals: the smallest eigenvector of the local covariance matrix Σₚ = Σᵢ (xᵢ − x̄)(xᵢ − x̄)ᵀ is taken as the surface normal, the other two eigenvectors span the tangent plane. That gives an SO(2)-bundle over the discrete pocket that the GEM kernels can act on without ever building a triangulated mesh.

6. The hard part: induced fit

A subtlety the model has to learn implicitly: a binding pocket is not a rigid lock. When the ligand approaches, both partners deform — induced fit. So the same protein can show up in different conformations across PDBBind entries, and the same ligand can adopt different conformers. PDBBind's bound co-crystal captures one frame from a thermal ensemble.

This is part of why pure rule-based scoring functions (AutoDock Vina, Glide) plateau around 1.4–1.5 RMSE in pKd: they assume a fixed pocket and a rigid scoring grid. A geometric deep network can in principle absorb the fluctuation patterns from data.

7. Why algebraic topology in particular

A binding pocket has multi-scale topological features that distance-based encoders are bad at capturing.

The pocket has voids — regions of the protein where the ligand is meant to fit. These appear as 1-cycles (H₁) in the Vietoris–Rips filtration as you grow ε.

Hydrophobic patches are unions of non-polar residues that may not be contiguous in sequence but are contiguous on the surface. They appear as persistent H₀ classes when the filtration is restricted to non-polar atoms.

Aromatic ring stacking (π-π interactions) leaves a characteristic short-lived H₁ class as ring atoms close into a cycle and then fill in.

Because Vietoris–Rips filtration depends only on pairwise distances, the resulting persistence diagram is SE(3)-invariant and permutation-invariant for free. The persistence-image vectorisation

ρ(b,p) = Σᵢ wᵢ · 𝒩((b,p); (bᵢ,pᵢ), σ²I)

(with weight wᵢ growing with persistence pᵢ = dᵢ − bᵢ) gives a fixed-length feature usable in an MLP. So the TDA branch contributes a complementary, basis-free fingerprint to the GEM-CNN's local geometric one.

8. Putting the biology back into the architecture

A compressed reading of models/toposurface_dti.py in biological terms:

The DrugEncoder answers "what is the small molecule, chemically and conformationally?" — it produces a 64-dim per-atom embedding that knows about atom type, hybridisation, and 3D layout up to rigid motion.

The PocketEncoder answers "what does the protein wall look like around this pocket?" — it produces a 64-dim per-residue embedding that knows about the surface's intrinsic geometry, including curvature signatures and orientation fields, but is invariant to which gauge you chose for tangent frames.

The TDA branch answers "what is the global shape of each object?" — voids, loops, connected components — independently of any local encoder choice.

The FusionModule answers "do these two shapes complement?" — cross-attention computes an alignment between drug atoms and pocket residues, and the TDA fingerprints are concatenated as global descriptors. This is the geometric analogue of asking whether the ligand's surface is the (local) inverse of the pocket's.

The output is a single scalar pKd that, by construction, is invariant under independent SE(3) actions on the two inputs and under arbitrary gauge choices on the pocket.

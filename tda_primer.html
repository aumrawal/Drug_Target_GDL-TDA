TopoSurface-DTI — Persistent homology primer

For a mathematical physicist with expertise in algebraic topology. Conversation saved 2026-05-12. Focus: the Vietoris–Rips filtration, the exact algorithm Ripser runs (apparent pairs + clearing lemma + cohomology duality), and persistence-image vectorisation. Maps to data/tda_features.py.

You already know simplicial homology and boundary operators, so I will skip what is standard and concentrate on what is specific to the model: which filtration is being computed, what Ripser is doing under the hood that makes it 100–1000× faster than a textbook implementation, and how the diagram becomes a fixed-length feature vector that the rest of the network can ingest.

0. Setup — from molecular point cloud to a chain complex

The TDA branch sees one object per pass: a point cloud X = {x₁, …, x_N} ⊂ ℝ³. For the drug this is the set of heavy atoms (≈ 10–60 points); for the pocket this is the set of Cα residue positions (≈ 30–200 points). The point cloud is unordered and we want a feature that depends on it only as a multiset in ℝ³ modulo SE(3) — i.e. permutation- and rigid-motion-invariant. Pairwise Euclidean distances are exactly the invariants of this action, so we build everything from the distance matrix:

D ∈ ℝ^{N×N},   D_{ij} = ‖xᵢ − xⱼ‖₂

From D alone the model recovers H₀ and H₁ of a filtered simplicial complex. Two intermediate facts you'll want in mind:

  • We work with F₂ = GF(2) coefficients throughout. The structure theorem for finitely generated modules over F₂[t]-with-grading still gives an interval decomposition; we just lose the torsion information we never used. Software (Ripser) standardises on F₂.
  • We only need H₀ and H₁. H₂ classes would require building 3-simplices, which Ripser will do if you ask but doubles the work. The code passes maxdim=1.

1. The Vietoris–Rips filtration

For ε ≥ 0 define

VR_ε(X) = { σ ⊆ X : diam(σ) ≤ ε }.

This is the maximal flag complex whose 1-skeleton is the ε-neighbourhood graph of X. ε ≤ ε' gives an inclusion VR_ε ↪ VR_{ε'}, hence a persistence module {Hₖ(VR_ε; F₂)} over the poset (ℝ≥0, ≤). The structure theorem decomposes it as a direct sum of interval modules; the multiset of intervals is the persistence diagram dgmₖ(X).

Two characteristic features show up in molecular point clouds:

  • H₀ classes all share birth 0 (every vertex is born at ε=0). Their deaths are the edge lengths in a minimum spanning tree of the complete graph on X. The longest-lived H₀ class is essential.
  • H₁ classes are born when a cycle of edges closes up without yet having a filling triangle, and die when the disk fills in. A binding pocket appears here: a curved arc of pocket-rim residues forms a 1-cycle at moderate ε before the pocket interior fills in at larger ε.

Things to notice as you sweep ε:

  • β₀ starts at 12 (one component per vertex) and decreases monotonically by 1 every time an edge merges two components. The set of edge lengths killing H₀ classes is exactly the MST edge set; this is Kruskal in disguise.
  • β₁ pops up when a non-bounding 1-cycle appears and disappears when a triangulation fills it in. For the noisy annulus there is typically one long-lived H₁ class corresponding to the hole, plus possibly a handful of short bars from local triangulation order.
  • "Long bars" (high persistence p = d − b) are the topologically robust features; "short bars" near the diagonal are noise. Persistence is exactly the multi-scale signal-to-noise filter we want.

2. The persistent (co)chain complex

Order the simplices σ₁, σ₂, …, σ_M by filtration value (entry time), breaking ties by dimension then by index. Form the boundary matrix

D ∈ F₂^{M×M}, D_{ij} = [σᵢ is a codim-1 face of σⱼ].

Column j of D is the cycle ∂σⱼ ∈ Cₖ₋₁ written in the basis {σ_i : dim σᵢ = k−1}. The standard reduction brings D into a form where each non-zero column has a distinct lowest non-zero entry (the "low" or pivot), via column additions over F₂:

R = D · V,   V upper-triangular invertible,  low(R_j) distinct across non-zero columns.

The pairing rule (Edelsbrunner–Letscher–Zomorodian 2002): for each non-zero column j of R with pivot i = low(R_j),

σᵢ is the birth, σⱼ is the death of a persistence pair.
Each unpaired non-zero column / non-pivot row gives an essential (infinite) bar.

The from-scratch fallback in _compute_tda_scratch implements exactly this: a dense GF(2) boundary matrix and the lowest-1 elimination. The algorithm is correct, but its cost is the killer. The naive reduction is O(M³) over F₂, where M is the simplex count. For a pocket with 100 residues the 2-skeleton has ∼10⁵ triangles — pure Python ≈ minutes per pocket. The Ripser core is the same algorithm but engineered to (i) never materialise most of D, (ii) skip most of the columns, and (iii) work on a sparser dual matrix.

3. What Ripser actually does

Three orthogonal tricks, all due to Bauer (Ripser: efficient computation of Vietoris–Rips persistence barcodes, 2021). They compose multiplicatively:

3a. Cohomology duality — work on coboundaries

For any filtered simplicial complex, persistent homology and persistent cohomology have identical barcodes (de Silva, Morozov, Vejdemo-Johansson 2011):

dgmₖ(H_*(VR_ε(X))) = dgmₖ(H^*(VR_ε(X))).

So you may instead reduce the coboundary matrix δ. The win is concrete: the coboundary matrix of a Vietoris–Rips complex is dramatically sparser than the boundary matrix, because a (k−1)-simplex has only a handful of cofacets in the complex but a k-simplex has k+1 facets and many of them. For VR specifically, the asymmetry can be orders of magnitude.

Concretely, Ripser enumerates simplices in reverse filtration order and reduces δ instead of D. The persistence pairs come out the same.

3b. The clearing lemma — skip zero columns

Bauer–Kerber–Reininghaus observation (2014): if simplex τ of dimension k+1 was paired with σ of dimension k during the dimension-(k+1) reduction, then when we move down to the dimension-k reduction, σ corresponds to a column that must reduce to zero. So in cohomology order (top-down), once a column has been "killed" at the higher dimension, we know in advance it contributes nothing at the lower dimension.

paired in dim k+1  ⇒  zero column in dim k  ⇒  skip.

For a typical Vietoris–Rips filtration up through H₁, this removes the vast majority of the 1-simplex columns from the H₀ pass — they have already been resolved while reducing the 2-simplex columns above them.

3c. Apparent pairs — most pairs are free

A zero apparent pair in a simplexwise filtration is a pair (σ, τ) where:

τ is the lexicographically-smallest cofacet of σ with filtration value equal to σ's,
and σ is the lexicographically-largest facet of τ with that same filtration value.

Bauer proved that any such (σ, τ) is a persistence pair of the reduction before running it. You can detect apparent pairs by a local check on each simplex — no global elimination required. In practice, > 95 % of all persistence pairs of a VR filtration are apparent. So Ripser:

  1. Enumerates simplices in coboundary order.
  2. For each, runs the local apparent-pair test in O(k) per simplex — and if it succeeds, emits the pair without touching the matrix.
  3. Only the remaining simplices (the essential tail) need real reduction, which now runs on a tiny coboundary submatrix.

The composite effect: a textbook O(M³) reduction becomes effectively O(M · α(M)) over the apparent-pair fraction plus a small dense residual. For molecular sizes this is the difference between seconds and tens of milliseconds per cloud — which matters because TDA features are recomputed for every datapoint at training time.

4. Persistence diagrams → fixed-length feature vectors

A persistence diagram dgm = {(bᵢ, dᵢ)}ᵢ is a multiset, not a vector, and neural networks want vectors. There is a wishlist for any vectorisation:

  1. Stable: small bottleneck-distance perturbations of dgm ⇒ small perturbations of the vector.
  2. Differentiable: smooth in (bᵢ, dᵢ).
  3. Fixed-length: independent of |dgm|.
  4. Linear: dgm ↦ vector is linear in the diagram (so superposition of independent regions composes additively).

Persistence images (Adams et al. 2017) satisfy all four. The recipe:

  1. Transform to (birth, persistence) coordinates: T(b, d) = (b, d − b). This sends the diagonal {b = d} to the axis {p = 0}, which is exactly where noise concentrates — important for the weighting in step 3.
  2. Place a 2D Gaussian of variance σ²I at each transformed point.
  3. Weight each Gaussian by w(p) — the codebase uses w(p) = p, so noise near the diagonal gets crushed.
  4. Evaluate the sum on a uniform resolution × resolution grid over [0, max_val]² and flatten.
ρ_dgm(b, p) = Σᵢ w(pᵢ) · 𝒩((b, p); (bᵢ, pᵢ), σ² I).

This is exactly the loop in persistence_image() (lines 71–76 of data/tda_features.py):

for b, p in zip(births, persists):
    if p < 1e-8: continue
    gauss_b = np.exp(-((gb - b) ** 2) / (2 * sigma ** 2))
    gauss_p = np.exp(-((gp - p) ** 2) / (2 * sigma ** 2))
    image  += p * np.outer(gauss_b, gauss_p)

The outer-product trick exploits the fact that an isotropic 2D Gaussian factors into a product of 1D Gaussians on each axis — turns the kernel evaluation from O(resolution²) into O(resolution) per point. The final image is normalised to [0, 1].

Two things worth flagging:

  • The codebase concatenates H₀ and H₁ images into a single 2 · 20² = 800-dim vector per molecule (tda_to_tensor). The drug and pocket each emit one such 800-dim vector, so the fusion module sees a 1600-dim TDA feature alongside the GEM-CNN and DrugEncoder embeddings.
  • The choice σ = max_val / resolution × 2 (line 65 of the code) is ≈ 2× the grid spacing — a Nyquist-ish smoothing that suppresses high-frequency artefacts of grid sampling without washing out structure. Persistence images are stable in 1-Wasserstein distance with constant linear in σ⁻¹; widening σ trades discrimination for noise robustness.

5. How this maps to TopoSurface-DTI

The signature of compute_tda_features is

compute_tda_features(points: (N,3),  max_edge_len: float = 8.0,  resolution: int = 20) -> dict

and it is invoked twice per training sample in dataset.py: once on the drug atom coordinates (max_edge_len=8 Å) and once on the pocket Cα coordinates (max_edge_len=16 Å, twice the drug). Why those values?

  • Drug max ε = 8 Å covers any ring (benzene diameter ≈ 2.8 Å), any local pharmacophore arrangement, and bridging conformations of small heterocycles. Anything beyond is essentially the molecule's global span, which adds little.
  • Pocket max ε = 16 Å is roughly the diameter of a binding pocket. The H₁ class corresponding to a closed pocket rim appears between ε ≈ 6 Å (consecutive Cα–Cα within a strand) and ε ≈ 12 Å (cross-pocket span). Going much further would start including non-pocket residues into spurious cycles.

The dict returned contains four entries:

{ 'h0_image': (400,) float32, 'h1_image': (400,) float32, 'h0_pairs': [(b,d)...], 'h1_pairs': [(b,d)...] }

The two images get concatenated by tda_to_tensor into the 800-dim feature used at train time. The raw pair lists are kept for diagnostics and visualisation only.

One last thing: the SE(3)- and permutation-invariance of the whole pipeline is now manifest. Distances are isometry invariants of ℝ³, the simplicial complex depends only on diam(σ), the persistence module depends only on the filtered complex, the diagram is a function of the persistence module, and the persistence image is a function of the diagram. So

π : X ↦ image(dgm(VR(X)))   is invariant under SE(3) × S_N acting on X.

This is the reason the TDA branch is paired with the GEM-CNN: GEM-CNN gives locally gauge-equivariant features that resolve pocket shape on the surface, and TDA gives globally rigid-motion-invariant features that capture multi-scale topology of the same point cloud. The two are complementary descriptors and the fusion MLP gets to learn the right linear combination.
